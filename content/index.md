This wiki documents the [[bioconomy|BioConomy]] framework: a coordination architecture for economies organized around [[bioregion|BioRegions]]. It exists to help those navigating the liminal space that inevitably occurs during periods of societal transition.

## Why?
The dominant economic system treats landscapes as inputs. It extracts soil, water, and biological complexity faster than they regenerate, and the institutional machinery that drives this (debt-based money and the shareholder corporation) has no built-in mechanism to stop. The results are visible everywhere: collapsing watersheds, hollowed-out rural economies, and a growing gap between the people who live in a place and the capital flows that shape it.

The BioConomy framework starts from a different premise: that the unit of economic organization should be the landscape itself and that the people coordinating within it can build structures that restore what extraction has drawn down. This is no theoretical proposal. Across 44 countries, place-based coordination initiatives are already operating at a bioregional scale. What they lack is a shared architecture for scaling without losing coherence.

## Why Now? 
### Two Phases of a Growth Curve

Every living system follows an [[s-curve|S-curve]]: slow growth at first, then rapid, then slow. The human body grows slowly as an embryo, rapidly as a teenager, and slows down during adulthood. The same pattern repeats in every living system, including the economic system every society depends on. 

The steep middle of the economic S-curve rewards [[throughput]]: rapid extraction, capital mobility, and expansion into new territory. Growth is the point, and the mechanisms that maximize flow (trade volume, GDP, return on capital) are structurally fit for that phase. This is how we've used the phrase "[[economy]]" since [[adam-smith|Adam Smith]] and [[david-ricardo|David Ricardo]]. It served its purpose, and it built the industrial world.

But the growth curve doesn't remain steep forever. As a system matures, the logic that suited acceleration begins to work against it. Extraction against limits degrades the capacity to reproduce. Maximizing flow drains the substrate that made flow possible. A mature system requires a different measure: value counted by what stays in place: soil, water, biodiversity, skill, community capacity, and cultural coherence. This is the BioConomy, and it's distinctly different from the Economy.

The developmental arc supporting this claim is centuries old. It runs from [[alexander-hamilton|Alexander Hamilton]] (1755-1804) and [[friedrich-list|Friedrich List]] (1789-1846) through [[abraham-lincoln|Abraham Lincoln]] (1809-1865) and [[thomas-sankara|Thomas Sankara]] (1949-1987), and the argument for why its time is now ripe is explored in [[concepts/bioconomy-developmental-arc|The BioConomy Developmental Arc]]. The full evidence base sits in [[research/growth-economics|the S-Curve research brief]].
