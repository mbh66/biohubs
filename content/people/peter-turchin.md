Russian-American evolutionary biologist and complexity scientist whose structural-demographic theory identifies the specific mechanisms of political instability in complex societies: elite overproduction, popular immiseration, and state fiscal distress. In a 2010 *Nature* paper, he correctly predicted the peak of political instability in the United States in the early 2020s.

## Contribution

Turchin's structural-demographic theory, tested across seven historical civilizations with quantitative data, identifies three interacting variables that produce secular cycles of political instability:

**Elite overproduction:** too many people competing for too few elite positions. The dynamics of intra-elite competition intensify as the ratio of aspirants to positions rises.

**Popular immiseration:** declining living standards for the majority. Real wages, life expectancy, and other indicators of population wellbeing decline as the elite share of resources grows.

**State fiscal distress:** governments unable to fund their commitments. Tax bases erode, debt accumulates, and the state loses capacity to respond to crises.

The three variables interact non-linearly. When all three are moving in the same direction, the probability of political instability rises sharply. Turchin's models, published before 2020, predicted a peak of political instability in the United States in the early 2020s. The prediction was correct.

For the [[frameworks/three-futures|Three Futures]] framework, Turchin's work provides the peer-reviewed empirical grounding for the [[frameworks/three-futures|Termination]] trajectory's specific mechanism. Where Ahmed's Planetary Phase Shift diagnoses the biophysical and technological dimensions, Turchin's structural-demographic theory diagnoses the political-institutional dimensions.
