First United States Secretary of the Treasury under President Washington, principal author of *The Federalist Papers*, and the intellectual originator of what later became the [[glossary/american-system|American System]] of political [[glossary/economy|economy]]. Hamilton was born in the West Indies, orphaned early, and rose through the Continental Army as Washington's aide-de-camp. His federal financial architecture (assumption of state debts, the First Bank of the United States, the tariff and revenue system) shaped the constitutional order in ways that outlived him.

Hamilton's *Report on the Subject of Manufactures*, delivered to Congress on 5 December 1791, is the foundational document of the [[retention-economics|Retention Economics]] tradition. It made the case for government promotion of domestic manufacturing through protective tariffs, direct bounties for infant industries, and federally funded internal improvements. Douglas Irwin's authoritative *Journal of Economic History* study (2004) documents that almost every tariff recommendation in the *Report* was adopted by Congress in early 1792, while the bounty (subsidy) recommendations failed. The tariffs Hamilton actually secured were moderate revenue duties, not highly protectionist ones, because he [[glossary/needed|needed]] import revenue to fund the public debt.

Hamilton belongs on this list as the paradigm case of [[structural-prematurity|Structural Prematurity]]. He was operating at the very base of the American industrial [[s-curve|S-curve]], before mechanized industry existed in the United States. Historians describe him as a quarter century ahead of his time. The productive capacity he sought to retain barely existed yet, and the political economy was dominated by the Southern agrarian export interest represented by Jefferson and Madison. That his ideas outlived his defeat, resurfacing in Clay's American System and Lincoln's Civil War program, is what makes him the anchor of the retention lineage rather than a footnote to it.
