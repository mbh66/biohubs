Hungarian economic historian and social theorist, educated in Budapest and Vienna, later a professor at Columbia University. His most influential work, _The Great Transformation_ (1944), was written during the collapse of the nineteenth-century liberal order and published as the postwar settlement was being negotiated. The book is one of the twentieth century's foundational critiques of market society, and it supplies much of the theoretical vocabulary that [[glossary/bioconomy|BioConomy]] draws on.

Polanyi's central argument was that the self-regulating market, presented by classical economics as a natural condition, is in fact a historically recent and structurally unstable arrangement. Human societies had for millennia embedded economic activity within social relations, reciprocity, and redistribution. The nineteenth century inverted this arrangement by embedding society within the market, treating land, labor, and money as commodities available for unrestricted trade. Polanyi called these the "fictitious commodities" because none of them are produced for sale. Land is nature. Labor is human activity. Money is a token of exchange or state finance. Treating them as commodities, Polanyi argued, would demolish the natural, human, and monetary substrate on which markets depend unless countervailing protections were built.

The book's second major contribution is the concept of the double movement. As market forces extend, society generates a protective counter-movement to defend itself against commodification. Labor movements, environmental regulation, banking controls, welfare states, and cooperative institutions are all expressions of this counter-movement. Polanyi observed the pattern in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. Contemporary retention economics, resource nationalism, food sovereignty movements, and [[glossary/bioregional-economics|bioregional economics]] can be read as expressions of the same pattern at planetary scale as the industrial [[s-curve|S-curve]]  enters deceleration.

Polanyi belongs on this list because BioConomy operates within his frame. The proposition that land, labor, and money must be re-embedded in social and ecological relations is the philosophical core of the [[glossary/regenerative|regenerative]] tradition. What Polanyi could not fully see, writing before the ecological sciences matured, was the planetary substrate itself as the outermost fictitious commodity. His framework extends naturally to it. The wiki treats Polanyi as the twentieth century's most important theorist of what happens when [[glossary/throughput|throughput]] logic is imposed on a substrate that cannot bear it, and as the first theorist to make the counter-movement legible as structural rather than merely political.
