An idea is structurally premature when it correctly diagnoses a problem but is offered against the prevailing gradient of a phase of the S-curve that selects for the opposite response.

The [[retention-economics|Retention Economics]] tradition provides the paradigm cases. Alexander Hamilton (1791) argued for domestic-industry protection before mechanised industry existed in the United States and was described by historians as a quarter century ahead of his time. Friedrich List (1841) theorised infant-industry protection and died impoverished a generation before Bismarck's Germany implemented it. Both were correct. Both were early. Both were structurally premature.

Structural prematurity is different from being wrong, and different from being defeated by contingent political opposition. A structurally premature figure is defeated by the gradient of the phase itself. The prevailing selection pressure runs against the diagnosis, and the practical policy that follows from it will be reversed, ignored, or bypassed even when it is materially superior to the alternative. The concept is what makes the [[frameworks/s-curve-thesis|S-Curve Thesis]] intelligible: the retention figures were not wrong; they were operating in a phase that could not reward them.

The falsification test is that frameworks judged structurally premature during acceleration should become structurally necessary during deceleration. If they do not, either the phase reading is wrong or the framework was mistaken on its merits.
